This catalogues traditions and how they arose. Nothing here asserts that any of these beings exist. The useful question is not “is it real?” but “where did this description come from, and did anyone arrive at it independently?” The cautions are recorded on the same basis: what a tradition warns is a fact about the tradition, and the risks worth taking seriously here belong to the practices, not to the beings.

For information only. This is a record of what traditions claim and where those claims came from — not instruction for practice, and not medical, psychological or spiritual advice. Three things are worth noticing. The structure appears in the early 1990s, not the 1950s — the contactees of the Adamski era had visitors, not a war. It maps cleanly onto human political narrative, with a council, a resistance and an occupying interest. And the roles are assigned by disposition rather than biology: the same Greys appear on both sides depending on which author is writing.

Compiled as a comparative catalogue of claims and their sources. Dates refer to publication or to the first fixed account, not to any event. Where a tradition has a single identifiable origin, that is stated; where it does not, that absence is itself the finding. Several traditions here — djinn, kami, nāgas, yakshas — are elements of living religions rather than curiosities, and are treated accordingly.
